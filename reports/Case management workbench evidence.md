# Clocks and capacity justify the workbench

A product manager can justify a Case Management Workbench MVP on three verified foundations, and should refuse to justify it on a fourth. The first is **regulatory and quality clocks**: three-attempt and 90-day rules for Special Needs Plan (SNP) members, 2- and 3-day transition windows, and a Star Ratings bonus pool of **at least $13.4B in 2026**. The second is **fragmented, stale data**: 68% of care managers at one plan rated multi-system inefficiency a major barrier, and only 43% of hospitals were routinely interoperable in 2023. The third is **measurable staff capacity**: a state rate model budgets about one hour of preparation and documentation per hour of member conversation. The claim to refuse is that the workbench will cut readmissions or medical cost, because large telephonic and population-scale programs have repeatedly failed randomized tests, and one randomized trial exposed a misleading 38-point naive decline. The evidence base has serious holes that the deck must show openly: this research found **no time-motion study of payer case managers, no national connect-rate benchmark, no payer case manager turnover rate, no published care management price per member per month (PMPM), and no public national percentage rates for the key HEDIS measures**. AI documentation evidence comes from clinicians in visits, and it shows modest savings (about 16-22 minutes a day), with omissions as the main failure. The right pitch is therefore a measured 90-day pilot with a concurrent control, centered on capacity, deadline compliance and auditability, in which the plan's own baseline replaces the missing benchmarks. Every number the deck uses should come from the Source register at the end of this report, with its evidence grade intact.

## Nobody has timed a payer case manager, so burden rests on a 2013 survey and a rate model

Caseload evidence supports the common planning ranges but not as a standard. No national benchmark could be read: CMSA, CCMC, NCQA and URAC material was paid or not retrievable, so this research cannot say whether those bodies prescribe a number. A vendor author reports a CMSA/NASW paper finding "average" caseloads of **2 to 365** across all settings ([CMSA Pulse](https://naylornetwork.com/cmsapulse/articles/print.asp?aid=258817)). The newest payer-employed number is a **median of 144 members** among care coordinators at Virginia's six Medicaid MCOs in 2022, though it comes from a substance-use study with a 24% response rate, and those coordinators are not stated to be all RNs ([AJMC](https://www.ajmc.com/view/challenges-faced-by-medicaid-managed-care-coordinators-working-with-members-with-substance-use-disorder)). The fullest plan-level table dates from 2011 and covers non-profit community plans ([ACHP](https://achp.org/wp-content/uploads/ACHP-Care-Management-Handbook.pdf)). Contract ceilings such as Iowa's **average of 45 and maximum of 50** apply to community-based LTSS work, not telephonic RN case management ([Iowa HHS](https://hhs.iowa.gov/media/15084/download?inline=)). North Carolina's ratios (29:1, 46:1, 87:1, 142:1) were built for rate setting only, count engaged members only, and assume extenders absorb part of the work ([NC DHHS](https://medicaid.ncdhhs.gov/updated-tailored-care-management-rate-guidance-jan-24-2022/download)).

| Commonly quoted range | Verdict | Best support | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Complex case management, 30-60 active cases per nurse | Supported; the highest-acuity programs run lower (10-29) | ACHP 2011; Iowa 45/50; NC 29:1 and 46:1; California ECM 20-50 | Medium: only ACHP is plan care manager data, and it is 15 years old |
| Telephonic disease management, 100-200+ | Supported as a range | ACHP 2011; Virginia median 144; NC 87:1 to 142:1 | Medium: Virginia and NC are not telephonic disease management specifically |
| Transitions of care, 60-100+ | Not validated; restate as stock versus flow | No payer source. The Care Transitions Intervention coach holds 24-28 at a time and about 300 a year, in a home-visit model | Low |

The transitions restatement matters. Sixty to 100 *concurrent* members is roughly 2-4 times the only verified model ([AHRQ PSNet](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/innovation/transition-coachesr-reduce-readmissions-medicare-patients-complex-postdischarge-needs)), while 60-100 *discharges a month* is arithmetically plausible: NC's assumptions yield about 143 productive hours a month, or about 71 discharges at two one-hour contacts each. The same calculation gives about 114 members for a monthly-contact panel, about 49 for a complex panel contacted every 14 days, and about 30 for a very high-touch panel. These are author calculations, but they show why caseload alone is a poor comparator: denominators differ (assigned versus engaged, work list versus open case), and contact cadence drives the arithmetic. The workbench should report caseload as hours of required work, not a head count.

On time use, **no time-motion, audit-log or diary study of payer case managers was found**. The closest payer-side figure is an assumption, not an observation: NC budgets **36% member contact and 36% travel, documentation and preparation**, with one hour per telephonic contact split evenly between talking and everything else ([NC DHHS](https://medicaid.ncdhhs.gov/updated-tailored-care-management-rate-guidance-jan-24-2022/download)). Measured data comes from hospital nurses and must carry a proxy label: 35.3% of practice time on documentation across 36 units in 2005-06 ([Hendrich](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3037121/)), and about 25% in a small 2018 observation ([PMC6371290](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6371290/)). The most useful study for pilot design shows the measurement gap inside one system: vendor audit logs recorded 157-162 minutes of EHR time per 12-hour shift (about 22%, an author calculation), while nurses self-reported about 43% on documentation and called 45% of it duplicative or unnecessary ([PMC9300261](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9300261)). Logs also miss off-EHR work, so a pilot needs both logs and observation. The frequently repeated "25-41% of shift" holds only partly and only for hospitals: no source supports the upper 41%.

The sole payer-specific systems study surveyed 80 of 131 care managers at a central Pennsylvania plan in 2012-13. All used case management software, 95% an EHR, 35% other claims and authorization systems and 26% an HIE. **68% rated duplicate entry and similar multi-system inefficiency a major barrier**, 60% cited incomplete HIE participation, and 49% cited multiple logins ([PMC6557451](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6557451/)). The same study carries the counterpoint that any honest deck must keep: after controls, obstacles explained **only 2-4% of the variance in workload, burnout and turnover intention, and were not significant**. Fixing systems therefore cannot be sold as a burnout cure on this evidence. Virginia points in a similar direction: coordinators reported high satisfaction and low burnout overall, with administrative and regulatory burden the barrier most strongly tied to burnout ([AJMC](https://www.ajmc.com/view/challenges-faced-by-medicaid-managed-care-coordinators-working-with-members-with-substance-use-disorder)). A vendor-commissioned survey adds perception data: 3 in 4 plan leaders see care managers acting more as information gatherers than trusted advisors ([PointClickCare](https://pointclickcare.com/blog/our-markets/health-plans/what-care-managers-could-be-doing-instead-of-chasing-charts/)). The seller of the fix paid for that survey, and it measures opinion, not time.

Turnover and replacement figures do not transfer. **No payer case manager turnover, vacancy or replacement cost was found.** NSI's 17.6% turnover and $60,090 per departure describe acute-care hospitals and include hospital backfill costs ([NSI](https://www.nsinursingsolutions.com/documents/library/nsi_national_health_care_retention_report.pdf)). What does anchor sizing is pay: RNs at insurance carriers had a **mean of $90,070, or $43.30 an hour, in May 2023**, the latest year read at industry level ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/oes/2023/may/naics4_524100.htm)). With NC's 29% benefit load this gives about $53-56 an hour. At about 2.9 hours a day of modeled preparation and documentation, the time pool is about $37k-$39k per case manager per year. That is an upper bound on addressable cost, not expected savings, and it includes travel for field staff.

## Reach has no benchmark, so the workbench must create one

The most important operational number for a case manager, the share of members actually reached, has no peer-reviewed national benchmark for plan care management. What exists is context. A vendor case reports connect rates rising from 22.52% to 65.78% in three months after adding branded caller ID and call authentication, uncontrolled and undated ([Carenet](https://carenethealthcare.com/?p=49991)). That makes it a cheap hypothesis to test with a holdout, not a target. A provider-run primary care program reached 61.5% with the full script, left voicemail for 29.0% and missed 9.5% among 790 discharges ([Tang et al.][epmc-tang]). A rural Medicare fee-for-service (FFS) program enrolled 62% of its intent-to-treat group ([Kranker](https://ajmc.s3.amazonaws.com/_media/_pdf/AJMC_05_2018_Kranker%20final.pdf)). Among Colorado Medicaid blind and disabled members, care managers "often required 10–20 calls to reach a member" ([Health Services Research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3796111)). These populations are too different to average. The Medicaid-versus-Medicare head-to-head comparison, optimal attempt count and best call time all lack quantified plan data. Qualitative plan advice does favor calling early in the month, when prepaid phones have minutes ([CMS Resources for Integrated Care](https://cms.gov/files/document/ricresource-locatingandengagingmembers-brief.pdf)).

Contact data quality explains part of the failure, and it is worst where it has been tested. A vendor could confirm only **about half of phones or addresses** among 80,016 Wisconsin FFS Medicaid households, though failure to confirm is not proof of error ([J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/impact-outreach-and-assistance-navigators-medicaid-renewals-united-states)). Only **38% of responding safety-net plans** called state contact data accurate most of the time ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/understanding-the-role-of-medicaid-managed-care-plans-in-unwinding-pandemic-era-continuous-enrollment-perspectives-from-safety-net-plans/)). **80.0% of US adults live in wireless-only households**, and 63.4% of adults 65 and over are personally wireless-only ([CDC/NCHS](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhis/earlyrelease/wireless202609.pdf)). These figures must not be extrapolated from Medicaid to Medicare or commercial members, and no national wrong-number rate exists. The product should therefore separate "not reachable because of bad data" from "reached and declined," as CMS's own counting logic does ([CMS FAQ](https://www.cms.gov/medicare-medicaid-coordination/medicare-and-medicaid-coordination/medicare-medicaid-coordination-office/financialalignmentinitiative/downloads/corereportingmeasure21faq_082516.pdf)), and report connect rates by preferred language, given evidence of a reach gap for limited-English patients ([Europe PMC][epmc-lep]). SMS evidence is mixed and from other settings: engagement split sharply in a safety-net hypertension study, and text and call arms produced about 3% vaccination among previously unresponsive adults ([Europe PMC][epmc-sms]). It does not justify a texting-first design, and under the SNP rule automated contacts do not count as attempts.

## Every dataset arrives late, partial or both

Data freshness is the workbench's strongest design argument, because a case manager who sees an undated field can't tell whether to trust it. The strongest source is a CMS Chronic Conditions Warehouse paper on Original Medicare claims for July 2023 services: about 44% of inpatient and 46% of physician claims are received in the service month, about 93% and 89% by the next month, and most claim types are about 95% final four months after service ([CCW](https://resdac.org/sites/default/files/2026-04/ccw-original-medicare-claims-part-d-events-maturity.pdf)). A caution on a phrase in circulation: "about 40-45% by the end of the service month" holds for inpatient, hospital outpatient and carrier claims only. SNF (1.59%), hospice (4.89%), home health (26.06%) and DME (57.03%) differ widely. **No equivalent curve was found for Medicare Advantage, commercial plans or Medicaid**, so plans must use their own lag triangles. The table below turns the research into a design rule for each source; the design column is an author recommendation.

| Dataset | Best verified freshness evidence | Best verified quality evidence | Evidence strength | Workbench design rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Claims | 92.56% inpatient, 89.40% carrier received by month 2 (Medicare FFS) | Inpatient primary diagnosis changes in 1.08% of month-1 first versions; payment in 5.19% | Strong for FFS; none for MA or commercial | Show service date, received date and "may still change" |
| ADT and event notices | Rule says "at the time of"; no measured latency | Minimum is patient, practitioner and institution name; plans are not named recipients | Rule strong; adoption and latency weak | Treat as a trigger, never as clinical content; flag missing diagnosis and disposition |
| Discharge summaries | 12-34% at first visit, 51-77% at 4 weeks (older review) | Pending tests missing in 65%; follow-up plans in 2-43% | Moderate, dated | Track "summary received" as a task with a clock |
| HIE, C-CDA, TEFCA | Query-based; over 2 billion records since Dec 2023 | 43% of hospitals routinely interoperable; under half of sampled patients had clinical-domain data | Moderate | Show source and conflicts side by side |
| Pharmacy | Part D first record 99.44%, final 80.34% by month 2 | 14-22% of levothyroxine users lost claims capture over four years | Moderate; PBM-feed latency unverified | Label "dispensed," never "taking"; confirm with the member |
| Labs | No verified latency | LOINC mis-mapping 4.6% of results (hematology 7.5%); administrative HEDIS rates about 20 points below hybrid | Moderate, HEDIS data old | Show a "result not received" state, separate from "not done" |
| Member contact | Static until updated | About half confirmable in one state; 38% of plans trust state data most of the time | Weak; no national rate | Log wrong numbers from outreach and verify on each contact |
| SDOH | Staleness not measured | Z codes on 1.59% FFS (2019), 1.07% MA sample (2019), 1.66% Medicaid (2022) | Strong for capture | Date every screening answer; a blank is not "no need" |
| Prior authorization | 72-hour and 7-day decisions since 2026 | Drugs excluded; APIs from 2027 | Strong on rule | Show status with last-updated time |
| Provider directory | 44.8% of bad Pennsylvania listings still wrong 117-280 days later | 52.20% of MA locations inaccurate (2016-17) | Strong, dated | Do not make a referral rest on the directory alone |
| Death data | 61% of deaths reported within 10 days (NCHS) | DMF in-hospital sensitivity fell from 88.9% to 14.8% after 2011 | Moderate | Never close a case on DMF alone |

Three of these caveats should reach the slide. The Kripalani discharge-summary figure comes from a 2007 review of mostly pre-EHR studies, so it probably overstates today's gap, and no national post-2020 measure was found ([Europe PMC][epmc-core]). ADT adoption has no verified number: the national share of hospitals sending notifications was not on any page opened, and the 84% and 42% figures from ONC measure electronic exchange and summary-of-care records, not ADT ([ONC](https://healthit.gov/data/data-briefs/interoperable-exchange-patient-health-information-among-us-hospitals-2023/)). TEFCA's volume does not establish payer usability, because no breakdown by exchange purpose was found ([HHS/ASTP](https://healthit.gov/?p=217794)). Medication data deserves the most caution, for a reason that later meets the AI evidence: post-discharge error rates range from 14.1% to 94% because the studies measure different things in different populations, so they should never be merged. Before CancelRx, only 34% of clinic-to-pharmacy discontinuations succeeded, which shows how a stopped drug can still look active ([Europe PMC][epmc-medlist]).

## Star money is large, but the workbench's share is about 17% and moving

The financial stake is verified and large. KFF puts 2026 quality bonus spending at **no less than $13.4B**, or 2.3% of projected MA payments, with 68% of enrollees in bonus-status plans; those figures are lower bounds because they assume an average risk score of 1, and MedPAC separately estimates about $16B ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicare/medicare-advantage-quality-bonus-payments/)). The 5% benchmark bonus switches on at 4 stars ([Wakely](https://www.wakely.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Wakely-Summary-2027-Final-Rule-Stars-Changes_Updated_2026-04-20.pdf)). In the 2027 ratings, published in October 2026, the enrollment-weighted average was 3.99, and 188 of 508 MA-PD contracts earned 4 or more stars ([CMS](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/2027-medicare-advantage-part-d-star-ratings)). Average stars show where the pain sits among relevant measures: **Follow-Up After ED Visit (FMC) fell to 2.5 and diabetes adherence to 2.7**, while Plan All-Cause Readmissions (PCR) rose to 3.4 and Transitions of Care (TRC) to 3.4.

The stake must not be overstated. Summing published weights, TRC, FMC, PCR and the three adherence measures total about **14 of about 81, or 17%** of MA-PD weight for 2027, an author calculation that assumes which two measures are duplicated across Parts C and D, and applicability varies by contract ([CMS](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/2027-star-ratings-measures.pdf)). The measure set is also moving. Medication Reconciliation Post-Discharge (MRP) left the Stars set in 2027, though it remains one of four TRC indicators, so the workbench should still run its clock. Wakely reports adherence weights falling from 3 to 1 in 2028 Stars and returning to 3 in 2029, and it reports SNP Care Management leaving in 2029, though the underlying SNP regulation stays. Wakely models 2029 average stars of 3.78 and a 1.2% payment reduction by 2030, but its 2027 forecast of 3.94 came in below the actual 3.99, so forecast error is real. The proposed rule's RFI also raised partly or fully separating Stars from bonus payments, which CMS has not done ([Wakely](https://www.wakely.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Wakely-Summary-2027-Final-Rule-Stars-Changes_Updated_2026-04-20.pdf)). A Stars business case that assumes constant weight across years would be wrong.

National percentage rates for TRC, FMC, PCR, FUH, FUM and adherence **could not be verified**, because NCQA gates them behind a form or Quality Compass, and the CMS data-table ZIP was not downloaded. The deck should use average stars, not percentages, until someone closes that gap. Measure cut points are likewise unreported, since the 2027 Technical Notes text could not be extracted. The definitions themselves, however, translate directly into product requirements. TRC runs on four clocks: admission notice and discharge information each within 3 total days, engagement within 30 days, and medication reconciliation within 31 total days ([NCQA](https://www.ncqa.org/report-cards/health-plans/state-of-health-care-quality-report/measures-list/transitions-of-care-trc/)). FMC runs on 7 days ([NCQA](https://www.ncqa.org/report-cards/health-plans/state-of-health-care-quality-report/follow-up-after-emergency-department-visit-for-people-with-high-risk-multiple-chronic-conditions-fmc/)). Two of the TRC clocks depend on data flow, not nurse effort, so the feeds described above determine whether a case manager can meet them at all. TCM's 2-business-day contact and 7- or 14-day visit windows are provider-billed, so a plan can support those clocks but does not itself bill the service ([AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/family-physician/practice-and-career/getting-paid/coding/transitional-care-management/faq.html)).

## Regulation writes the requirements, with two deadlines 8 and 12 weeks away

Regulatory evidence is the firmest in this report, and it reads almost like a backlog. The **federal SNP rule** requires an initial HRA within 90 days of the enrollment effective date, annual repeats, and at least three attempts, not counting automated calls, on different days and at different times of day, followed by a letter if there is no response, with attempts and refusals documented ([42 CFR 422.101(f)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/422.101)). Medicaid managed care requires "best effort" screening within 90 days without a fixed attempt count in the text read ([42 CFR 438.208](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/438.208)). A commonly assumed NCQA three-attempt rule **could not be verified**, because NCQA's standards are sold, so the deck should credit the three-attempt rule to CMS. Attempt policy should be a configurable rule by line of business, with state rules layered on top.

Two dates are close, measured from 6 November 2026. **The CMS-0057-F Patient Access, Provider Access, Payer-to-Payer and Prior Authorization APIs generally apply from 1 January 2027**, with dates varying by payer type; 72-hour and 7-day decisions have applied since January 2026 ([CMS](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/cms-interoperability-and-prior-authorization-final-rule-cms-0057-f)). **TCPA's revoke-all requirement is delayed to 31 January 2027**, and the FCC is reviewing whether to change it ([FCC](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-26-12A1.pdf)). Revocations must be honored within 10 business days ([47 CFR 64.1200](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/64.1200)). That same page links an amendment at 91 FR 67219, dated 26 October 2026, whose effect was not determined, and the healthcare exemption text refers to a "healthcare provider," so counsel must decide whether plan-initiated outreach qualifies. Part 2 compliance has applied since 16 February 2026, allowing a single consent for treatment, payment and operations but keeping protections against use in proceedings against the patient ([42 CFR 2.33](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/2.33)). Section 1557 reaches tools that guide care and disease management, which plausibly includes risk stratification, and it requires qualified human review of machine translation when accuracy is essential ([45 CFR 92.210](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/92.210); [45 CFR 92.201](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/92.201)). Its federal enforcement posture in 2025-26 was not verified.

AI rules converge on one principle: tools can assist, but adverse decisions require individual circumstances and a qualified human. CMS's 2024 FAQ, read through a law-firm summary, says predictions alone cannot justify a coverage decision ([Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/cms-confirms-medicare-advantage-organizations-may-use-ai-in-making-coverage/)), and nine more states have laws on AI in coverage, downcoding or adverse-decision reporting taking effect in 2026-28 ([Rise Health](https://www.risehealth.org/insights-articles/ai-health-insurance-state-laws-2026/)). In Estate of Lokken v. UnitedHealth, breach-of-contract claims survived and the court compelled broad discovery in March 2026, but no merits ruling was found, and the "over 90%" reversal figure is a plaintiffs' allegation, not a finding ([ArentFox Schiff](https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/alerts/federal-court-orders-broad-discovery-against-uhc-ai-coverage-denial-lawsuit); [BankInfoSecurity](https://www.bankinfosecurity.com/court-unitedhealth-must-answer-for-ai-based-claim-denials-a-27534)). These laws target utilization review. A workbench stays outside them only if its outputs do not feed adverse determinations, so the boundary should be technical, not just policy. All of this section is a design input, not legal advice.

## Case management works in person and in small trials, not at call-center scale

The effectiveness record splits by model, and it should decide what the deck may promise. Intensive, nurse-led, in-person transitional care shows real effects in older trials: Naylor 1999 cut 24-week readmission from 37.1% to 20.3% ([Europe PMC][epmc-ma]), Coleman reported 8.3 versus 11.9 rehospitalization at 30 days, with units not stated in the retrieved summary ([Europe PMC][epmc-coleman06]), and a 42-trial meta-analysis pooled to a risk ratio of 0.82 for 30-day readmission, with pre-2002 trials 1.6 times more effective ([Leppin][epmc-trials]). Remote and population-scale programs tell the opposite story. Medicare Health Support randomized 242,417 people to nurse call-center programs and found no reduction in admissions or ED visits, with about $400M in fees and no demonstrable savings ([RTI](https://www.rti.org/publication/results-medicare-health-support-disease-management-pilot-program)). Thirteen of 15 Medicare care coordination programs showed no significant hospitalization difference, and none generated net savings ([Peikes][epmc-peikes]). Colorado's telephone care management RCT, with 5,064 members, found no significant utilization differences ([HSR](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3796111)).

The Camden trial supplies the sharpest lesson for product claims: 180-day readmission was 62.3% versus 61.7%, while a naive pre/post comparison showed a **38-point decline** ([NEJM via Europe PMC][epmc-camden]). Any before-and-after ROI slide, including one for this product, would inherit that flaw. The steelman for case management remains real. Atlantic General's program reduced spending by $1,333 per beneficiary per month (90% CI -$2,078 to -$589) without significant change in readmission, in a quasi-experimental design with modest power ([Kranker](https://ajmc.s3.amazonaws.com/_media/_pdf/AJMC_05_2018_Kranker%20final.pdf)). HIP reported net savings of $4,965 per participant, but with 101 self-selected participants ([Picariello](https://scholars.mssm.edu/en/publications/impact-of-a-geriatric-case-management-program-on-health-plan-cost-2/)). TCM billing was associated with lower 31-60-day mortality and cost, but readmissions differed by only 0.2 points in an observational design with likely selection effects ([Bindman & Cox](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6583218)). Few MA plan-run RCTs reporting readmissions were found, and CCTP's final results could not be read.

The workable position follows from this. A software workbench does not turn a telephonic program into Naylor's model, so faster follow-up should be presented as a process and compliance outcome. Medical cost reduction belongs in a later, properly controlled evaluation. Medicare readmission context is 15.7% in MA against 15.4% in FFS unadjusted, for July 2018 to June 2019 ([Europe PMC][epmc-ma]); it shows the size of the base rate and is old enough to need a date on the slide. The cost per readmission remains unverified, because HCUP pages refused access, so the deck should not price avoided readmissions at all until a person confirms the figures.

## AI drafts save tens of seconds a note and fail by leaving things out

The best trials show modest time savings. A UCLA three-arm RCT found Nabla reduced time in note from 4:30 to 3:49, against about 18 seconds of improvement in controls, with DAX not significant ([UCLA](https://www.uclahealth.org/news/release/ucla-study-finds-ai-scribes-may-reduce-documentation-time)). A multistate stepped-wedge trial found 0.36 fewer note hours a day and exhaustion down 0.44 on a 5-point scale ([Consultant360](https://www.consultant360.com/exclusive/ambient-ai-scribes-linked-lower-work-exhaustion-multistate-pragmatic-trial)). A five-hospital observational study found about 16 fewer documentation minutes a day (10%) but **no change in after-hours EHR time**, and only 32% of users used the tool in more than half of visits ([Mass General Brigham](https://news.massgeneralbrigham.org/en/ai-scribes-linked-to-modest-reductions-in-ehr-documentation-time)). One search summary suggests the "per day" unit may be normalized to 8 scheduled patient hours, so check the JAMA paper before using that wording. Kaiser's "15,791 hours saved" works out to about **22 seconds per encounter**, close to UCLA's net of about 23 seconds, and that estimate comes without accuracy data ([AMA](https://www.ama-assn.org/practice-management/digital-health/ai-scribes-save-15000-hours-and-restore-human-side-medicine)). Burnout results look more consistent than time results, although the 51.9% to 38.8% figure comes from an uncontrolled, Abridge-linked pre-post study with self-reported after-hours data ([Olson](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2839542)).

Errors set the design. Across five platforms and simulated encounters, 26.3% of key elements were omitted or wrong, **omissions were 76.3% of note errors**, and medication errors were the most common type ([Anderson](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12605248/)). LLM discharge summaries averaged 2.91 errors against 1.82 for physicians, with equivalent overall quality and no significant difference in potential harm ([Williams via The Hospitalist](https://www.the-hospitalist.org/hospitalist/article/40519/in-the-literature/llms-and-physicians-generate-comparable-hospital-discharge-summaries/)). A preprint's GPT-4o F1 falls from **94.0% for medication extraction to 72.7% when discontinuation status is included** ([Shao](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11137v2)), and current status is exactly what a case manager needs. A reviewer cannot spot what is absent from a draft without the source, so the interface must link every statement to its source and flag what it did not find. Alert evidence points the same way: 49-96% of drug-safety alerts were overridden in a 2006 review, many of them appropriately ([van der Sijs](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540)), and the Epic Sepsis Model's PPV of 12% means about 8 alerts per true case ([JAMA Intern Med](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2781313)). No override data for payer workflows exists, so this is an extrapolation, but the sensible response is fewer, severity-tiered prompts with published precision.

|---|---|---|
| AI does, spot-check | Ingest and normalize records; find documents; pre-fill demographics; encounter timeline; outreach logistics | Low clinical judgment; vendor accuracy claims must be re-measured on plan data |
| AI drafts, clinician edits and signs | Chart summary, case notes, call summary, suggested care plan goals | Omissions expected; source links required |
| Clinician confirms each item | Active medications and changes, allergies, new diagnoses, risk statements, social needs, member preferences | Medication status is the weak spot (F1 72.7%, preprint) |

The transfer caveat is decisive: **no study of LLM drafting for payer case management was found**, nor any study of LLM medication reconciliation on real US EHR data. A case manager's work is chart review, outreach and care planning rather than visit notes, so savings could be larger or smaller. A simple sanity check shows why this matters. If a 10% saving did transfer to the 2.9-hour modeled pool, it would free about 17 minutes a day, worth about $3.7k-$3.9k a year per case manager or about two more members on a 49-member complex panel. That is less than one Salesforce Health Cloud Core seat at list price, which is $4,200 a year. Documentation drafting of scribe-level size does not by itself pay for a platform, so the MVP also has to reduce chart assembly and failed outreach.

## Incumbents own the record, and nobody has published payer proof

The competitive field is more crowded than typical vendor lists show, and its reviews conflict. KLAS's public 2025 table ranks ZeOmega Jiva 82.2 (21 organizations), Elligint Health VirtualHealth HELIOS 80.4 (6), MHK CareProminence 77.6 (12), HealthEdge GuidingCare 76.3 (11) and Zyter TruCare 73.9 (11), with Cognizant CareAdvance 83.1, Epic Tapestry 81.5 and Medecision Aerial 49.5 listed but unranked on small samples ([KLAS 2025](https://klasresearch.com/best-in-klas-ranking/care-management-solutions-payer/2025/306)). Cognizant won 2026 at 83.7, with the rest of that table behind a login ([KLAS 2026](https://klasresearch.com/best-in-klas-ranking/care-management-solutions-payer/2026/306)). Medecision Aerial scored 49.5 from 3 organizations at KLAS in 2025, then ranked #1 of 25 at Black Book in 2026 ([Black Book](https://www.newswire.com/news/managed-care-and-health-plan-clients-rate-medecision-1-for-care-management-case)). Neither should be treated as settled, and the two must never be blended. Several common errors also need fixing. "Casenet/Altruista TruCare" merges two products: Altruista's platform is GuidingCare, while Casenet's TruCare went to Zyter. Jiva is no longer the reigning Best in KLAS. Thoughtful AI sells revenue-cycle agents and appears out of scope.

The market's size and structure are clear; budgets are not. MA holds **35.2M of 64.2M eligible beneficiaries in 2026**, with UnitedHealth and Humana at 46% ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicare/medicare-advantage-in-2026-enrollment-update-and-key-trends/)). Medicaid MCOs held **over 66M as of July 2024**, across 291 MCOs, with five parents at 47% ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/10-things-to-know-about-medicaid-managed-care/)). Adding them gives about 100M lives with dual-eligible overlap and mismatched years, and the Medicaid figure has probably declined since. **No public PMPM price, care-manager-per-1,000-members ratio or care management spend benchmark was found.** The only list prices are seats: Salesforce Health Cloud at $350-$725 per user per month ([Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/healthcare-life-sciences/health-cloud/pricing/)). Buyer identity rests on two press releases quoting clinical executives and a CIO, which fit but do not establish a clinical sponsor with CIO co-approval, so 3-5 interviews must validate it. A rising Medicaid MLR (91% in 2024) suggests tight administrative budgets and may favor a layer over a replacement, but that is an inference. Nothing read proves that plans prefer an overlay.

The white space is proof. Payers are spending heavily on member and call-center AI, but no published, verifiable result on case manager time savings at a large payer was found. Black Book reports that 75% of payer technology users want human review, explainability, monitoring and audit trails before broad deployment, and that buyers want cycle-time and defect evidence rather than disconnected pilots ([Black Book](https://www.newswire.com/news/black-book-polls-find-health-plans-moving-from-digital-transformation-22789286)). For HiLabs specifically, the solutions page shows no care or case management product and no "DataGuard" ([HiLabs](https://www.hilabs.com/our-solutions)). Its right to win would be installed plan relationships and clinical data ingestion (MCheck Clinical), with the company reporting six of the top ten plans, unnamed ([HiLabs release](https://digital-release.wowktv.com/business/press-releases/cision/20260812PH24778/hilabs-named-to-the-inc-5000-list-for-the-second-consecutive-year)). The risk is that none of its published proof concerns clinician users. If the audience is not HiLabs itself, all its figures need "HiLabs reports" attribution.

## The evidence points to a narrow MVP and a controlled 90-day pilot

The recommended first release is an overlay for RN case managers on one line of business, starting with Medicare Advantage including D-SNP. That is the author's judgment: MA has explicit federal attempt rules, published Star weights and a verified bonus pool, whereas Medicaid has worse contact data and a stronger reach problem but state-by-state rules. Medicaid is a defensible alternative, and interviews should test it. Utilization management stays out entirely.

The pilot proposal, which no source sets as a norm, is 90 days at one plan and line of business with 20-50 case managers, a 2-4 week baseline, and a stepped or concurrent control, because UCLA's control arm improved by itself and Camden's naive comparison misled by 38 points. Comparable evaluations ran from 30 days to 24 weeks, none of them in payer SaaS. Readmissions and cost should be observed, not promised. The stop rule tied to major error rate should be agreed before launch.

## Conclusion

The research changes what the case should be. The intuitive pitch, that burned-out nurses drown in paperwork and that better software prevents readmissions, is the least supported version: the one payer study finds obstacles explain little burnout variance, Virginia's coordinators report low burnout, and telephonic programs have failed at scale. The better-supported pitch is less dramatic and easier to defend: a plan's case managers face hard clocks using late, partial data, and no one can currently show what fraction of their time or attempts is wasted. Measurement itself is therefore part of the product. A plan that adopts the workbench would hold baseline figures this research could not find anywhere, and a vendor that publishes controlled payer results would have proof no competitor has shown publicly.

The clearest risk to the business case is arithmetic. Scribe-sized savings appear too small to pay for a list-priced seat on their own, and the Stars-linked share of weight is about 17% and shifting. The pilot should therefore be judged on whether capacity gain exceeds scribe-level effects, through chart assembly and outreach as well as drafting. If it does not, the honest fallback is a compliance and audit product priced accordingly, or a data-layer feature inside incumbents' systems of record, not a standalone workbench.
